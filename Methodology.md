## Team Selection

Five teams selected to represent a spread of public front-office reputations,
in order to test whether the data supports or contradicts conventional wisdom:

- **Bad cap management:** Saints, Browns
- **Good cap management:** Eagles, Chiefs
- **Middle of the road:** Cardinals

## Team Selection Rationale

Team reputations for cap management can shift year to year, so it matters
that the "good," "bad," and "middle" labels below are grounded in the
specific era this project studies — roughly 2021-2025, when the 2017-2019
draft classes were moving through their years 5-7 transition window — rather
than current-day reputation alone.

**Saints — bad cap management.** The clearest case in the pilot. Without a
cost-controlled rookie quarterback to ease the cap, the Saints signed
veteran quarterbacks to expensive deals in this window (Jameis Winston in
2022, Derek Carr in 2023) and relied heavily on restructuring existing
contracts — converting salary into signing bonus to create short-term cap
space at the cost of pushing larger bonus proration into future years. Their
2021 extension with CB Marshon Lattimore was itself restructured in 2022,
2023, and 2024, a pattern that fits squarely into this project's "extended,
then dealt with dead cap consequences later" outcome type. Independent
analysis (PFF, 2023) ranked the Saints' roster near the bottom of the league
in cap space and total prorated money during this stretch.

**Browns — bad cap management, but a different failure mode.** The Browns'
cap trouble in this window is driven almost entirely by one contract — the
fully guaranteed Deshaun Watson deal signed in 2022 — rather than a broad
pattern of poor extension or drafting decisions across the roster. This is
worth distinguishing from the Saints' more systemic restructure-heavy
approach: both are "bad cap management," but one is a single outlier
contract distorting the sheet, the other is a repeated organizational habit.
Findings involving the Browns should account for this when drawing
conclusions about their transition-window decisions specifically.

**Eagles — good cap management.** The Eagles' reputation for cap efficiency
was earned specifically during this window, built around a core of largely
homegrown, well-timed extensions (Kelce, Johnson, Hurts, Brown) that
culminated in a 14-3 season and Super Bowl appearance in 2022. This overlaps
directly with the project's pilot data on Eagles extensions (Sweat, Goedert,
Maddox, Mailata).

**Chiefs — good cap management.** The Chiefs sustained multiple deep
playoff and Super Bowl runs through this period while continuing to
re-sign their own transition-window players, making them a useful
comparison case for "extended on time, and it worked out."

**Cardinals — middle of the road, flagged as a caveat.** Unlike the other
four teams, there isn't a strong independent analyst narrative — positive or
negative — specifically covering Arizona's cap management in this window.
Their inclusion as "middle" is provisional rather than externally confirmed,
and should ultimately be validated by the project's own data (i.e., a mixed
record of good and bad outcomes among their 2017-2019 draft class) rather
than treated as settled by an absence of press coverage. This is noted as an
open item to revisit once Cardinals data is collected.
## Transition Window Definition

The "post-rookie transition window" is defined as **years 5–7 of a player's career**.

Rookie contracts typically run 4 years (5 for first-round picks whose 5th-year
option is exercised), so years 5–7 represent the first stretch after the rookie
deal ends and before a second, larger contract typically begins around year 7–8.
This window is where front offices make their highest-leverage decisions:
extend, franchise tag, trade, or let the player walk.

**Outcome categories tracked for each player in the window:**
- **Extended (on time)** — signed a second contract during or at the end of
  the window, in the normal timeframe.
- **Extended early** — signed before year 5, still on the rookie deal.
- **Franchise tagged** — retained via franchise tag rather than a long-term deal.
- **Traded** — moved to another team during the window, in exchange for
  what the front office deemed better value (picks, players, or cap relief).
- **Let walk (free agency)** — allowed to leave with no compensation returned.

Each category is treated as a distinct decision type rather than collapsed
into a single "kept vs. lost" bucket — a trade and a walk-away both result in
losing the player, but only one returns value, so they need to be scored
differently.

**Edge cases:**
- Players who leave the league before year 5 (retirement, injury, etc.) are
  excluded from the transition-window pool.
- Players extended early are compared against players who played out the full
  rookie deal as a dedicated sub-finding.

## Data Collection & Player Selection

The initial dataset pulled for the Eagles (the project's first team) included
over 260 players spanning multiple decades and acquisition types — draft
picks, undrafted free agents, trades, and veteran free-agent signings dating
back to 2002. This was narrowed down through several passes:

1. **Scoped to draft classes 2017–2019** — the only window where a player's
   full years 5–7 transition window is complete as of 2026, per the
   Transition Window Definition above.
2. **Restricted to players who entered the league through the Eagles
   specifically** — drafted by Philadelphia or signed as an Eagles UDFA.
   Players acquired later via trade or free agency from other teams were
   removed, since their rookie-contract decision belongs to whichever team
   originally signed them, not the Eagles.
3. **Verified against primary sources** (Pro Football Reference, team beat
   reporters, Spotrac, Over The Cap) to correct data-entry errors and confirm
   true original signings, removing players who were mistakenly included
   despite being drafted elsewhere (e.g. Gardner Minshew, Kerryon Johnson).

This left **55 confirmed original Eagles players** (drafted or UDFA,
2017–2019) as the base population. From there, contract research is being
conducted only on players who **actually reached year 5** with the Eagles,
since players cut, released, or out of the league before that point never had
a transition-window decision to analyze. Players who left the Eagles *before*
reaching year 5 (e.g. traded or waived early) are noted separately as
out-of-scope for the core metric, but flagged for potential mention as
context in findings.
